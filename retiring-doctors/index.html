Will your doctor retire soon?

In Germany, 32% of practising doctors were 55 or over in 2024: the 14th oldest of 27 EU countries (Luxembourg has no 2024 figures). At today's rate, its medical schools will turn out 0.8 graduates in the next 10 years for every doctor aged 55 or over.

Notes

Most countries report practising doctors: those who treat patients. Some report all professionally active doctors (including those in administration or research) or everyone licensed to practise, which includes retired doctors and makes the older age groups larger. Eurostat's age table doesn't say which count a country reports; this page finds it by matching each country's total to Eurostat's health personnel table, and marks countries that don't report practising doctors with an asterisk (Slovakia and Türkiye: professionally active doctors; Greece: doctors licensed to practise). Values Eurostat marks estimated, a break in series or a different definition are labelled so in the tooltip and the table. Luxembourg has no 2024 figures (its latest are from 2012); it is listed without bars and not ranked.

Graduates are people who completed medical school in the country in a year, averaged over 2022–2024. Not every graduate stays: some countries train many students from abroad, and some lose young doctors to other countries, while others rely on doctors trained elsewhere. The ratio of graduates to doctors aged 55 or over shows whether a country trains enough doctors to replace those nearing retirement, not whether it will keep them. It also ignores that a growing and ageing population needs more doctors than today. Eurostat publishes no age breakdown by speciality, so the page can't show how old general practitioners are.

Italy counts all practising doctors only since 2021; before, it counted doctors in its national health service, of whom 54% were 55 or over. The page shows one year only, so breaks like this don't affect it.
