Source: Rx1 Eurostat · hlth_silc_08, Rx2 Eurostat · hlth_silc_09

  • In the EU-27, 2.4 in every 100 people went without a medical examination they needed in 2025, because it cost too much, was too far away or had a waiting list.
  • Across the EU-27, this happened most often in Greece (11.5 in every 100) and least often in Cyprus and Malta (0.1).
  • In 18 of the 27 countries of the EU-27, waiting lists were the main reason people went without.
  • Across the EU-27 as a whole, waiting lists kept the most people from a medical examination they needed (1.2 in every 100).
  • In Germany, 1.0 in every 100 people went without a medical examination they needed, fewer than across the EU-27 (2.4).
  • In Germany, the most common reason people went without was waiting lists.

The same share for the poorest fifth of the population by household income (hollow ring) and the richest fifth (filled amber dot), with each number beside its dot. Countries are ranked by the gap between them, largest first. Tap or click a row to pick a country.

Source: Rx1 Eurostat · hlth_silc_08, Rx2 Eurostat · hlth_silc_09

  • In the EU-27, people in the poorest fifth went without 2.6 times as often as people in the richest fifth.
  • Among the countries of the EU-27, the gap between the poorest and the richest fifth was largest in Greece: 18.9 against 4.9 in every 100 went without.
  • In 25 of the 27 countries of the EU-27, the poorest fifth went without more often than the richest fifth.
  • Among the countries of the EU-27, the poorest and the richest fifth were closest in Slovenia (3.3 against 3.2 in every 100).
  • In Germany, 1.3 in every 100 people in the poorest fifth went without, against 0.8 in the richest fifth.
  • So in Germany, the gap between the poorest and the richest fifth is smaller than across the EU-27.

Notes

The figures come from the EU survey on income and living conditions (EU-SILC). People aged 16 or over are asked whether, in the last 12 months, they needed a medical (or dental) examination or treatment and didn't get it, and for the main reason. The page shows Eurostat's headline measure: people whose main reason was that it was too expensive, too far to travel or there was a waiting list, the reasons a health system can do something about. Other reasons (no time, fear of doctors or treatment, waiting to see whether it gets better) are not included. The page shows each reason as Eurostat publishes it, and the total beside them; all are rounded to one decimal, so the reasons don't always add up exactly to the total.

It counts care people went without, not how long they waited for it: someone who waits weeks for a specialist and then sees one doesn't count. A country with long waits for appointments can therefore still have a low share, if people wait rather than go without.

Income fifths: everyone is ranked by their household's disposable income, adjusted for the size of the household, and split into five groups of equal size. The survey covers people in private households, not in care homes. Small shares rest on few answers, so differences of a few tenths of a percentage point are within the survey's margin. What public insurance covers differs between countries, dental care above all, which shows in the dental figures. Eurostat flags some 2025 values: Lithuania (provisional), Luxembourg (break in series).
